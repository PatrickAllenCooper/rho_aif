How much should an agent pay to satisfy its curiosity?

Thrilled to share that our paper "Expected Free Energy as Belief-Dependent Utility for rho-POMDPs" was accepted as a Spotlight at the International Workshop on Active Inference (IWAI) 2026.

The problem: agents acting under uncertainty must decide when to gather more information and when to act on what they know. Standard practice bolts an "exploration bonus" onto the reward and tunes its weight by trial and error, per environment. Get it wrong in one direction and the agent commits blindly; wrong in the other and it tests forever.

Our result: active inference already contains the answer. We prove that minimizing Expected Free Energy is exactly equivalent to solving a rho-POMDP where reward is augmented with information gain at weight 1. That weight is not a hyperparameter. It falls out of the variational bound, because information gain and reward end up measured in the same units (nats).

And it works. Across ten benchmark environments, from the classic Tiger problem to RockSample and a new structural inspection task with 65,536 hidden states:

- The derived weight of 1 lands at or near the reward-maximizing knee of the Pareto curve in every environment, with zero tuning.
- On diagnosis-style tasks it beats same-horizon reward-only planning on success rate and reward simultaneously.
- The advantage grows with scale: on an 8x8 search grid, reward-only planning collapses to 2.5 percent success while the EFE agent holds 66.5 percent.

The animation below shows the idea in one episode. Two agents, same maze, same sensors. The reward-only planner has no reason to value what a scan teaches it, so it searches inefficiently and commits to the wrong cell. The active inference agent treats information as worth exactly one nat per nat, narrows its belief, and commits correctly.

Curiosity has a price, and you do not have to tune it.

Full paper and code coming soon. Grateful to the IWAI community for the recognition.
