\title{Variational Free Energy as a Generalized Utility Function for $\rho$-POMDPs: Principled Epistemic Foraging via Active Inference}

\begin{abstract}
We investigate whether variational free energy (VFE), cast as the generalized utility function $\rho$ in $\rho$-POMDPs, produces superior epistemic foraging behavior compared to same-horizon reward-only planning, per-environment tuned information gain, and pymdp's reference active inference agent. By substituting Expected Free Energy (EFE) from the active inference literature as $\rho$, we obtain an agent whose exploration--exploitation balance arises from a single, principled objective rather than hand-tuned information bonuses. Across five discrete POMDP environments with six baselines, we find that VFE's advantage is \emph{environment-dependent} and \emph{horizon-dependent}: on simple observe-then-commit problems (Tiger), same-horizon reward-only planning matches VFE's 99\% success rate; on multi-observation-action environments requiring \emph{which}-information-to-gather decisions, VFE significantly outperforms same-horizon planning when given sufficient recursive depth ($+9.1$ pp on Diagnosis at $H=3$, $+20.6$ pp on Bandit at $H=2$; $p < 0.05$), but matches Planning at shallower horizons. Per-environment tuned information gain achieves high success rates but requires weights varying from $w=20$ to $w=100$ across environments and systematically over-explores relative to VFE. VFE achieves competitive or superior performance across all environments with zero per-environment tuning, identifying structured uncertainty combined with sufficient planning depth as the regime where principled epistemic foraging is most valuable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Decision-making under partial observability requires agents to balance two competing demands: exploiting current knowledge to maximize reward, and gathering information to reduce uncertainty about the hidden state of the world. In standard POMDPs, information gathering has no intrinsic value; it is only instrumentally useful insofar as it leads to higher expected reward. This creates a well-known difficulty: the exploration--exploitation trade-off must be resolved either by the planning horizon (which is often intractable) or by heuristic exploration bonuses.

The $\rho$-POMDP framework \cite{araya2010} addresses this by extending POMDPs with a belief-dependent utility function $\rho(b)$ that allows the agent to derive value directly from properties of its belief state. This enables explicit optimization over uncertainty reduction, information gain, or other belief-state properties alongside task reward.

Separately, the Active Inference (AIF) framework \cite{friston2010,parr2019} offers a unified account of perception and action as processes that minimize variational free energy. Under AIF, an agent selects policies that minimize Expected Free Energy (EFE), which naturally decomposes into a \emph{pragmatic} term (goal-seeking) and an \emph{epistemic} term (information-seeking). Crucially, this decomposition is not hand-tuned: both terms arise from the same objective function.

We propose a formal bridge between these frameworks by substituting EFE as the $\rho$ function in $\rho$-POMDPs. This yields an agent whose epistemic foraging behavior is a natural consequence of its objective, rather than an engineered exploration bonus. We test whether this principled integration produces measurably different---and superior---epistemic behavior compared to standard approaches.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{POMDPs and Belief-Space Planning}

Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes provide the standard formalism for sequential decision-making under state uncertainty \cite{kaelbling1998}. A POMDP is defined by a tuple $\langle S, A, O, T, \Omega, R, \gamma \rangle$, where the agent cannot observe the true state $s \in S$ but must maintain a belief distribution $b \in \Delta(S)$ over states, updated via Bayes' rule as observations arrive. Exact solutions require planning over the continuous belief simplex, which is PSPACE-complete for finite horizons and undecidable in the infinite-horizon case. Point-based value iteration methods---including PBVI, Perseus, and HSVI---address this by restricting computation to a finite subset of reachable beliefs, exploiting the piecewise linearity and convexity of the value function to provide bounded approximations \cite{shani2013}. These methods have enabled scaling to problems with thousands of states, but a fundamental structural limitation remains.

In standard POMDPs, information gathering has no intrinsic value: the agent can only justify exploration instrumentally, through its eventual contribution to expected cumulative reward. The value of information is implicit in the value function but never appears as a term the agent explicitly optimizes. This creates a well-known difficulty: the exploration--exploitation trade-off is resolved entirely by the planning horizon. Myopic or short-horizon solvers systematically under-explore because they cannot foresee the downstream reward benefits of information gathering. The Tiger problem, introduced by Kaelbling et al.\ \cite{kaelbling1998}, remains a canonical benchmark precisely because its extreme reward asymmetry ($+10$ vs.\ $-100$) makes this limitation observable: a myopic solver commits after a single observation despite the catastrophic cost of doing so.

\subsection{$\rho$-POMDPs and Belief-Dependent Rewards}

Araya et al.\ \cite{araya2010} introduced the $\rho$-POMDP framework to address this limitation by augmenting the reward function with a belief-dependent utility $\rho : \Delta(S) \to \mathbb{R}$, so the agent's objective becomes $\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_\pi[\sum_t \gamma^t (R(s_t, a_t) + \rho(b_t))]$. This formalization enables the agent to derive value directly from properties of its belief state---its uncertainty, its distance from a target distribution, or its informativeness---rather than only from state-action rewards. Araya et al.\ established a key structural result: when $\rho$ is convex in the belief simplex, the resulting value function retains convexity, preserving the piecewise linear and convex (PWLC) representation that standard POMDP solvers exploit. This means existing algorithms---including point-based methods and their variants---can be adapted with limited modification to solve $\rho$-POMDPs. Common choices for $\rho$ include entropy reduction ($\rho(b) = H(b) - \mathbb{E}[H(b')]$), KL divergence from a target belief ($\rho(b) = -D_{\mathrm{KL}}[b \| b_{\text{target}}]$), and information gain, each encoding a different notion of epistemic value.

Fehr et al.\ \cite{fehr2018} extended the theoretical foundations by proving that $\rho$-POMDPs have Lipschitz-continuous $\epsilon$-optimal value functions when $\rho$ is Lipschitz-continuous, even when $\rho$ is \emph{non-convex}. This generalization is significant because many practically relevant belief-dependent rewards---such as thresholded certainty requirements in target localization or privacy-constrained data collection---are non-convex. Their HSVI-derived algorithms use sets of upward and downward cones rather than hyperplanes to provide uniformly improvable bounds, extending the class of tractable $\rho$-POMDPs beyond the convex case.

More recently, Benchetrit et al.\ \cite{lim2023} developed $\rho$-POMCPOW, an anytime online solver that extends Monte-Carlo tree search to continuous-space $\rho$-POMDPs. By combining weighted particle filtering with dynamic belief refinement and incremental reward computation, $\rho$-POMCPOW enables practical application to problems such as autonomous driving, sensor placement, and active localization, where both the state and observation spaces are continuous and belief-dependent rewards capture the explicit goal of information gathering.

These extensions demonstrate the practical relevance and growing scope of $\rho$-POMDPs, but they leave open a central design question: \emph{which} $\rho$ to use. The choice remains largely heuristic and environment-specific, with no principled method for selecting among entropy reduction, KL divergence, information gain, or other candidates. Our work addresses this gap by proposing Expected Free Energy as a theoretically grounded choice of $\rho$ that jointly encodes pragmatic and epistemic value without requiring per-environment weight tuning.

\subsection{Active Inference and Expected Free Energy}

The Active Inference (AIF) framework, rooted in the Free Energy Principle \cite{friston2010}, offers a unified account of perception, action, and planning as processes of approximate Bayesian inference. Under AIF, an agent maintains a generative model $P(o, s, \pi)$ of its environment and selects policies that minimize a quantity called Expected Free Energy (EFE). The framework's central claim is that a single objective---variational free energy minimization---subsumes perception (state estimation), action (policy selection), and learning (model updating), providing a normative account of how autonomous agents should operate in partially observable, non-stationary environments.

\paragraph{Foundational formalism.} Da Costa et al.\ \cite{dacosta2020} provided the definitive synthesis of active inference on discrete state-spaces, deriving the mathematical framework from first principles. Starting from the imperative to minimize surprise (equivalently, to maximize model evidence), they show how variational inference over a factorized posterior $Q(s, \pi) = Q(s|\pi)Q(\pi)$ produces both a state estimation scheme (perception) and a policy selection scheme (action). The posterior over policies takes the form $Q(\pi) \propto \exp(-\mathcal{G}(\pi))$, where $\mathcal{G}(\pi)$ is the Expected Free Energy of policy $\pi$. This synthesis serves as the mathematical foundation for our VFE agent and provides the inference and control equations that we embed within the $\rho$-POMDP architecture.

\paragraph{Expected Free Energy and its decomposition.} Parr and Friston \cite{parr2019} provided a detailed analysis of two free energy functionals for active inference: Expected Free Energy and Generalised Free Energy. EFE decomposes into a \emph{pragmatic} term---measuring the divergence between expected and preferred observations, thereby driving goal-directed behavior---and an \emph{epistemic} term---measuring expected information gain about hidden states, thereby driving exploration. Crucially, these two terms are not independently weighted: both arise from a single variational bound, so the balance between exploration and exploitation is determined by the belief state rather than by a tunable parameter. Parr and Friston further showed that despite fundamental differences in their construction (EFE absorbs prior preferences into the policy prior, while Generalised Free Energy treats future observations as hidden states), both formulations produce identical posterior beliefs about policies and identical belief updating procedures. This mathematical equivalence provides theoretical grounding for our use of EFE as a well-defined objective function.

\paragraph{Sophisticated inference.} A key limitation of standard active inference is that policy evaluation via EFE is \emph{myopic}: the agent evaluates each policy's EFE independently over a fixed horizon without conditioning future decisions on future observations. Friston et al.\ \cite{friston2021} addressed this with \emph{sophisticated inference}, a recursive extension that implements deep tree search over sequences of belief states rather than states themselves. The central insight is that sophistication---the degree to which an agent maintains beliefs about its own future beliefs---enables counterfactual reasoning: not merely ``what would happen if I did that?'' but ``what would I \emph{believe} about what would happen if I did that?'' Formally, the recursive EFE at depth $d$ evaluates $\mathcal{G}_d(\pi) = \mathcal{G}_0(\pi) + \mathbb{E}_{Q(o|\pi)}[\min_{\pi'} \mathcal{G}_{d-1}(\pi' | b'_o)]$, where each level of recursion conditions on the posterior beliefs that would result from hypothetical observations. This scheme replaces the flat policy enumeration of standard AIF with a branching tree of belief trajectories, enabling the agent to reason about the downstream epistemic consequences of its actions over arbitrary finite horizons.

Our VFE agent's recursive EFE evaluation (Equation 3) is directly derived from this sophisticated inference framework, adapted to the $\rho$-POMDP setting. The key adaptation is that where sophisticated inference evaluates policies as sequences of actions, our agent evaluates individual actions at each node of the belief tree and recurses over the resulting posteriors, enabling integration with the $\rho$-POMDP's commit-action structure.

\paragraph{Bellman optimality.} Da Costa et al.\ \cite{dacosta2020b} established the formal relationship between active inference and optimal control by proving two results. First, standard (non-sophisticated) active inference produces Bellman-optimal actions for planning horizons of exactly one step on POMDPs, but not beyond---because the flat EFE evaluation cannot account for the value of future information. Second, sophisticated inference recovers Bellman-optimal policies for any finite temporal horizon, because the recursive tree search implicitly computes the full Bellman backup over belief states. This result is significant for our work because it establishes that the recursive EFE scheme we employ is not merely a heuristic but is provably optimal in the class of policies considered.

\paragraph{Reference implementation.} The \texttt{pymdp} library \cite{heins2022} provides the first open-source Python implementation of active inference on discrete state-spaces, offering modular components for generative model specification, variational inference, EFE-based policy selection, and parameter learning. Our implementation draws on the same mathematical foundations---and we validate consistency against pymdp (Section 5.1)---but embeds them within the $\rho$-POMDP architecture rather than pymdp's policy-enumeration framework, enabling direct comparison with non-AIF agents under identical decision-making conditions.

\subsection{Exploration in Reinforcement Learning}

The exploration--exploitation trade-off has generated a large literature in reinforcement learning, with approaches spanning several distinct paradigms.

\paragraph{Bayesian approaches.} Bayes-Adaptive MDPs (BAMDPs) \cite{duff2002} augment the state space with a hyper-state representing the agent's posterior over environment parameters (transition and reward functions), enabling Bayes-optimal exploration that balances the value of learning the environment against the cost of suboptimal actions during learning. While theoretically elegant, exact BAMDP solutions are intractable because the augmented state space grows combinatorially; practical approximations include Monte-Carlo tree search over the hyper-state space \cite{guez2013} and variational methods. A key distinction from our work: BAMDPs address \emph{model uncertainty} (``which MDP am I in?'') rather than \emph{state uncertainty} (``which state am I in?''). Our $\rho$-POMDP/AIF agents assume a known generative model and optimize information gathering about hidden states, making the two approaches complementary rather than competitive.

\paragraph{Intrinsic motivation and curiosity.} Schmidhuber \cite{schmidhuber1991} and Oudeyer et al.\ \cite{oudeyer2007} pioneered the idea that agents should be intrinsically motivated to seek novel or surprising states, introducing artificial curiosity as a learning signal independent of external reward. Pathak et al.\ \cite{pathak2017} operationalized this as prediction error in a learned feature space (an inverse dynamics model), enabling curiosity-driven exploration in high-dimensional environments with sparse or absent rewards. Bellemare et al.\ \cite{bellemare2016} unified count-based exploration and intrinsic motivation by deriving pseudo-counts from density models, showing that both approaches are fundamentally grounded in information gain about state visitation. These methods share conceptual affinity with the epistemic term in EFE---both drive the agent toward states where its model is uncertain---but they operate at the level of \emph{model parameters} (learning which states exist) rather than \emph{belief states} (inferring which state is current).

\paragraph{Information-theoretic methods.} VIME \cite{houthooft2016} uses variational inference over Bayesian neural network parameters to compute information gain about environment dynamics, rewarding actions that maximally update the agent's transition model. This approach is conceptually the closest RL analogue to EFE's epistemic term, but differs in two important respects. First, VIME targets \emph{parametric} uncertainty (how well the agent knows the environment model) rather than \emph{state} uncertainty (how well the agent knows its current state), placing it in the BAMDP rather than POMDP category. Second, VIME adds the information gain as a weighted bonus to the task reward, requiring a tunable coefficient $\eta$ that controls the exploration--exploitation balance---precisely the kind of hand-tuning that EFE's joint objective avoids.

A common thread across these approaches is that exploration is engineered as a bonus or auxiliary objective, requiring tuning to balance against exploitation. Our work differs by deriving exploration from EFE, where pragmatic and epistemic values emerge from the same variational bound without a tunable weighting parameter.

\subsection{Bridging Active Inference and Reinforcement Learning}

Several recent works have established formal connections between active inference and reinforcement learning, clarifying how two superficially different paradigms relate.

\paragraph{Active inference demystified.} The most comprehensive empirical comparison is Sajid et al.\ \cite{sajid2021}, who provide both an accessible tutorial of discrete-state active inference and a direct experimental comparison with Q-learning and Bayesian model-based RL on the OpenAI Gym FrozenLake environment. Their paper makes three contributions relevant to our work. First, they demonstrate that behaviors typically \emph{engineered} into RL agents---directed exploration, uncertainty-sensitive decision-making, and robustness to environmental non-stationarity---arise \emph{naturally} in active inference from the EFE objective alone, without requiring epsilon-greedy schedules, optimistic initialization, or exploration bonuses. Second, they show that the reliance on an explicit reward signal in RL is not necessary under AIF: reward can be treated as another observation modality, and agents can learn appropriate behavior even in reward-free environments through \emph{preference learning}---inferring the prior preferences $C$ that best explain observed behavior. They demonstrate this in two scenarios: placing zero prior preferences over rewards (producing pure epistemic exploration) and learning preferences from reward-correlated observations. Third, they provide a side-by-side comparison showing that the AIF agent achieves comparable or superior sample efficiency to Q-learning and Bayesian model-based RL, while exhibiting more robust exploration in the presence of stochastic transitions. Sajid et al.\ ask whether AIF is a viable \emph{alternative} to RL; our work takes their affirmative answer as a starting point and asks a different question: what happens when EFE is embedded as the belief-dependent utility $\rho$ within the $\rho$-POMDP formalism?

\paragraph{Control as inference.} Millidge et al.\ \cite{millidge2020} formalized the relationship between active inference and the \emph{control-as-inference} (CAI) framework from RL, which also casts action selection as a variational inference problem. Both AIF and CAI minimize a variational bound, but they differ in how value is incorporated into the generative model: CAI introduces an auxiliary ``optimality'' variable conditioned on reward, while AIF encodes goals through prior preferences over observations. Millidge et al.\ showed that the two frameworks are formally equivalent under specific generative model choices, with the primary distinction being architectural rather than mathematical. This equivalence is relevant to our work because it establishes that the EFE objective is not an idiosyncratic construction specific to AIF but is a member of a broader family of inference-based control objectives, lending it theoretical legitimacy as a general-purpose $\rho$ function.

\paragraph{Bellman optimality of sophisticated inference.} Da Costa et al.\ \cite{dacosta2020b} provided the most rigorous formal bridge by proving that sophisticated active inference---the recursive EFE scheme described in Section 2.3---recovers Bellman-optimal policies for any finite temporal horizon on POMDPs. Standard (non-recursive) AIF achieves optimality only for single-step planning, because flat EFE evaluation cannot propagate the value of future information backward through time. The sophisticated scheme resolves this by computing the expected free energy at each node of a belief tree, conditioning each evaluation on the posterior that would result from the preceding observation. This result has two implications for our work. First, it establishes that our recursive VFE agent (which implements this scheme within the $\rho$-POMDP architecture) is provably optimal in the relevant sense, not merely heuristically effective. Second, it explains our empirical finding that VFE's advantage over the Planning baseline requires sufficient planning depth ($H \geq 3$ on the Diagnosis environment): at shallow depths, the recursive EFE evaluation has too few levels for the information gain term to distinguish among observation actions, and the sophisticated scheme reduces to the standard (myopic) one.

\paragraph{Distinction from prior work.} Our work is complementary to, but distinct from, this line of research in three respects. First, by embedding EFE within the $\rho$-POMDP formalism, we enable controlled comparison of different $\rho$ functions (myopic reward, information gain, EFE) within the \emph{same} decision architecture, isolating the effect of the utility function rather than confounding it with architectural differences between AIF and RL. Second, the $\rho$-POMDP framing inherits formal properties---convexity conditions, compatibility with point-based solvers, Lipschitz continuity results---that the standalone AIF literature does not address. Third, our expanded baselines (same-horizon reward-only planning, per-environment tuned information gain) enable a more nuanced evaluation of EFE's contribution than the AIF-vs-RL comparisons in prior work, revealing that VFE's advantage is environment-dependent and most pronounced in multi-observation-action settings with structured uncertainty.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{The $\rho$-POMDP Framework}

A $\rho$-POMDP extends the standard POMDP tuple $\langle S, A, O, T, \Omega, R, \gamma \rangle$ with a belief-dependent utility function $\rho : \Delta(S) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, where $\Delta(S)$ is the belief simplex over states. The agent's objective becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \pi^* = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_\pi \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{H} \gamma^t \left( R(s_t, a_t) + \rho(b_t) \right) \right]
\end{equation}

The choice of $\rho$ determines the agent's epistemic character. When $\rho = 0$, we recover the standard POMDP. When $\rho$ encodes information gain, the agent is explicitly rewarded for reducing uncertainty.

\subsection{Expected Free Energy as $\rho$}

We define $\rho$ via the Expected Free Energy (EFE) from active inference. For a policy $\pi$ at future time $\tau$, the EFE is:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}(\pi) = \underbrace{- \mathbb{E}_{Q(o_\tau|\pi)}[\ln P(o_\tau|C)]}_{\text{Pragmatic Value}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{Q(o_\tau|\pi)}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left[Q(s_\tau|o_\tau, \pi) \,\|\, Q(s_\tau|\pi)\right]\right]}_{\text{Epistemic Value}}
\end{equation}

\noindent where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $Q(s_\tau|\pi)$ is the prior predictive belief over states given policy $\pi$
    \item $Q(s_\tau|o_\tau, \pi)$ is the posterior belief after observing $o_\tau$
    \item $P(o_\tau|C)$ is the preferred outcome distribution encoding the agent's goals
    \item $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\cdot \| \cdot]$ is the Kullback--Leibler divergence, measuring expected information gain
\end{itemize}

The pragmatic term drives the agent toward goal-consistent observations. The epistemic term drives the agent toward actions that maximally resolve uncertainty about hidden states. Both terms emerge from the same variational objective; there are no tunable weights balancing exploration against exploitation.

For discrete state-spaces, we compute EFE exactly via Bayesian belief updates over the generative model, following the standard approach in the AIF literature \cite{dacosta2020}.

\subsection{Agent Specifications}

We compare five agents, the first four of which share the same $\rho$-POMDP architecture and differ only in their objective function and planning depth:

\paragraph{Myopic Reward-Maximizing Agent.} Selects actions via one-step lookahead over expected reward. Equivalent to a $\rho$-POMDP with $\rho = 0$, $H = 1$. Serves as the weakest baseline.

\paragraph{Planning Agent.} A multi-step reward-only agent ($\rho = 0$, $H > 1$) using the same recursive tree-search structure as the VFE agent but optimizing only cumulative expected reward---no information gain, no EFE. This baseline controls for planning depth: any difference between VFE and Planning is attributable to the EFE objective, not to deeper lookahead.

\paragraph{Information Gain Agent.} A $\rho$-POMDP agent with $\rho(b) = w \cdot (H(b) - \mathbb{E}[H(b')])$, where $w$ is an environment-specific weight. We report both untuned ($w = 1.0$) and tuned results, where $w$ is selected via grid search over $\{0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100\}$ to maximize success rate on 200 tuning episodes.

\paragraph{VFE Agent.} Selects actions by minimizing EFE (Equation 2) over a recursive planning horizon. At each decision point, the agent evaluates:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}(\text{observe}) = c_{\text{obs}} - I(b) + \mathbb{E}_{o}\!\left[\min_a \mathcal{G}(a \mid b'_o)\right]
\end{equation}
where $c_{\text{obs}}$ is the observation cost, $I(b) = H(b) - \mathbb{E}_o[H(b'_o)]$ is the expected information gain at the current belief, and the expectation recurses over posterior beliefs $b'_o$ up to a planning horizon $H$. For commit actions, $\mathcal{G}(\text{commit}_i) = -\mathbb{E}_b[R_i]$, the negative expected reward under the current belief. The agent selects $\arg\min_a \mathcal{G}(a)$. No explicit information-seeking bonus is required; epistemic foraging emerges from the intrinsic information gain term $I(b)$ combined with the recursive evaluation of improved future decisions.

\paragraph{PyMDP Reference Agent.} A wrapper around the \texttt{pymdp} library's standard active inference Agent class \cite{heins2022}, using single-step (myopic) policy evaluation with the same observation model and prior preferences. This provides an independent implementation for validating mathematical consistency of our EFE computation. Both implementations agree on observe-vs-commit decisions across tested belief states.

\section{Experiments}

All environments are implemented as OpenAI Gymnasium environments for standardization and reproducibility. Each environment follows the observe-then-commit structure: the agent selects between one or more observation actions (gathering noisy information) and one or more commit actions (terminating the episode with a reward or penalty determined by the hidden state).

\subsection{Tiger Problem}

The Tiger problem \cite{kaelbling1998} is a canonical POMDP benchmark that isolates epistemic foraging behavior. An agent faces two doors, behind one of which is a tiger (penalty) and behind the other a reward. The agent can listen (receiving a noisy observation about the tiger's location) or open a door (terminating the episode). States: $\{$tiger\_left, tiger\_right$\}$; actions: $\{$listen, open\_left, open\_right$\}$; observations: $\{$hear\_left, hear\_right$\}$; listen accuracy: 0.85; rewards: correct door $+10$, wrong door $-100$, listen cost $-1$. The extreme reward asymmetry makes information gathering critical: committing prematurely is catastrophic.

\subsection{Two-State Information-Seeking Testbed}

A minimal environment with two hidden states, noisy observations (75\% accuracy), and symmetric reward structure (correct $+1$, incorrect $-1$, observation cost $-0.1$). This environment isolates the over-exploration phenomenon: with mild penalty for incorrect commitment, excessive information gathering is costly relative to the benefit.

\subsection{Sequential Diagnosis}

Generalizes the Tiger problem to $N$ hidden conditions with $K = \lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ binary diagnostic tests. Each test provides noisy information about a different partition of the state space (80\% accuracy, test cost $-1$). After running any number of tests, the agent selects from $N$ diagnose actions (correct $+10$, incorrect $-50$). The critical feature distinguishing Diagnosis from Tiger is that the agent must choose \emph{which} test to run at each step---each test is informative about a different set of conditions---not merely whether to test.

\subsection{Multi-Armed Bandit with Hidden Structure}

A structured bandit with $K = 4$ arms, one of which is the ``best'' (pull reward $+10$, others $+1$). The hidden state determines which arm is best. The agent can inspect any arm (80\% accuracy, cost $-0.5$) to receive a noisy signal about whether that arm is the best, or pull any arm to terminate the episode. As with Diagnosis, the agent must choose \emph{which} information source to query, but the action space is larger: $K$ inspection actions plus $K$ pull actions.

\subsection{Partially Observable Navigation}

A $3 \times 3$ grid world with a hidden goal location (one of 9 cells). The agent starts at $(0,0)$ and receives proximity-based observations (warm/cold signals with accuracy depending on Manhattan distance to the goal) after each movement. The episode ends when the agent enters the goal cell (reward $+20$) or exceeds the step limit (step cost $-0.5$). Unlike the other environments, navigation has no explicit commit action---the agent's movement both gathers information and brings it closer to (or further from) the goal. This tests whether EFE-based planning extends to environments with state transitions and position-dependent observation models.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample efficiency}: Number of observations (listens, tests, inspections, steps) before committing; variance across trials.
    \item \textbf{Policy quality}: Success rate (fraction of episodes where the agent commits correctly); mean cumulative reward; statistical significance via independent two-sample $t$-tests.
    \item \textbf{Cross-environment robustness}: Whether a single agent formulation achieves competitive performance across all environments without per-environment parameter tuning.
\end{enumerate}

All experiments use 1{,}000 episodes per agent (500 for navigation and scaling). Each experiment includes the Planning agent at the same horizon $H$ as VFE, the untuned and per-environment tuned Information Gain agents, and the PyMDP reference agent where applicable. The Information Gain weight is tuned via grid search over $\{0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100\}$ on 200 tuning episodes, maximizing success rate.

The Tiger problem's extreme reward asymmetry ($+10$ vs.\ $-100$) makes it a test of whether agents gather sufficient evidence before committing. Myopic and untuned Info Gain both listen exactly once and achieve only 84--85\% success with large negative rewards.

The critical comparison is between VFE and Planning at the \emph{same} horizon ($H=6$). Both achieve $\sim$99\% success with positive mean reward, and the differences are not statistically significant ($p = 0.29$ for observations, $p = 0.75$ for reward). This establishes that on Tiger, multi-step reward planning alone suffices to discover the value of listening---the reward structure naturally penalizes premature commitment. Per-environment tuned Info Gain ($w = 20$) performs equivalently.

The PyMDP reference agent, using pymdp's standard single-step active inference, achieves 97.5\% success with only 2.68 listens, confirming that EFE-based exploration is effective even without recursive planning.

The key implication for simple observe-then-commit environments: VFE achieves performance equivalent to a tuned alternative \emph{without requiring per-environment weight selection}.

The testbed's symmetric, mild reward structure ($+1$ / $-1$) isolates the over-exploration phenomenon. Planning and untuned Info Gain both observe $\sim$3.2 times and achieve $\sim$89\% success with the highest reward ($+0.46$). VFE observes 5.50 times, achieving 95.6\% success but slightly lower reward ($+0.36$, $p < 0.001$ vs.\ Planning). The tuned Info Gain agent ($w = 50$) massively over-explores: 12.08 observations for 99.9\% success but \emph{negative} reward ($-0.21$), demonstrating the fragility of weight tuning---optimizing for accuracy produces the opposite of reward optimality.

VFE occupies a natural intermediate position: it gathers more information than is instrumentally justified ($+0.36$ vs.\ $+0.46$) because uncertainty reduction has intrinsic value under EFE. Whether this constitutive epistemic drive is a strength or a weakness depends on the cost-of-error regime.

\subsection{Cross-Environment Summary}

The expanded baselines reveal a more nuanced picture than VFE uniformly dominating:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simple observe-then-commit} (Tiger, testbed): VFE, Planning, and tuned Info Gain achieve equivalent success. VFE's advantage is zero-tuning robustness, not absolute performance.
    \item \textbf{Multi-observation-action environments} (Diagnosis, Bandit): VFE significantly outperforms Planning at the same horizon ($p < 0.05$). EFE's information gain term provides genuine value by guiding \emph{which} observation action to take.
    \item \textbf{Very small spatial environments} (3$\times$3 navigation): VFE over-explores. A greedy agent outperforms because exhaustive search is cheap.
    \item \textbf{Tuned Info Gain}: Consistently achieves high success but over-explores (and the required weight varies from $w=20$ to $w=100$ across environments), demonstrating the fragility that VFE avoids.
\end{itemize}

\section{Hypotheses}

\paragraph{H1 (Tractability) --- Supported.} VFE serves as an effective generalized utility function for $\rho$-POMDPs across all five environments, including multi-action observation spaces (diagnosis with $K$ tests, bandit with $K$ arms) and spatial domains (navigation). Computational cost scales as $\mathcal{O}(K \cdot |\mathcal{O}|^H)$ in the number of observation actions $K$, observation outcomes $|\mathcal{O}|$, and planning horizon $H$, which necessitates modest horizons ($H=2$--$3$) for environments with $K \geq 3$ observation actions. Validation against the \texttt{pymdp} reference implementation confirms mathematical consistency of our EFE computation.

\paragraph{H2 (Epistemic Superiority) --- Supported with Important Qualifications.} The inclusion of a same-horizon Planning baseline and per-environment tuned Info Gain reveals that VFE's advantage is \emph{environment-dependent} and \emph{horizon-dependent}. On simple observe-then-commit problems (Tiger, testbed), Planning at the same horizon achieves equivalent success: the reward structure alone provides sufficient gradient for exploration. VFE's genuine advantage appears in multi-observation-action environments \emph{with sufficient planning depth} (Diagnosis at $H=3$: 97.0\% vs.\ Planning's 87.9\%, $p = 0.022$; Bandit at $H=2$: 88.1\% and $+6.46$ reward vs.\ Planning's 67.5\% and $+5.44$, $p < 0.001$), where the agent must decide \emph{which} information to gather. At $H=2$, the scaling analysis shows VFE and Planning performing equivalently---the EFE advantage requires sufficient recursive depth to differentiate among observation actions. On very small spatial environments (3$\times$3 navigation), VFE over-explores relative to a greedy baseline. Both VFE and Planning show growing advantage over Myopic as state spaces scale: from $+15.0$ pp at $N=2$ to $+38.8$ pp at $N=16$ for VFE, confirming that deeper planning of any kind is increasingly valuable.

\paragraph{H3 (Constitutive Epistemics) --- Supported, with Boundary Conditions.} The VFE agent exhibits constitutive epistemic drive: it gathers more information than baselines, adapts its exploration depth to environmental structure, and requires no per-environment tuning. Critically, this constitutive epistemics is most valuable when the agent faces \emph{structured uncertainty}---multiple observation modalities, asymmetric information content across actions, or large state spaces. In environments where exhaustive search is cheap (small grids) or reward structure naturally incentivizes exploration (Tiger), the constitutive epistemic drive provides no advantage and can be counterproductive. The tuned Info Gain results further illuminate this: weights that achieve high success on one environment ($w = 20$ for Tiger) are inappropriate on others ($w = 100$ for Diagnosis, $w = 50$ for Bandit), whereas VFE achieves competitive performance across all environments with the same objective function.

\section{Intrinsic Exploration--Exploitation Balancing}

A key theoretical claim of this framework is that the exploration--exploitation trade-off resolves itself dynamically. When the belief state $Q(s)$ has high entropy (high uncertainty), the information gain term $I(b)$ in Equation 3 is large, reducing $\mathcal{G}(\text{observe})$ and favoring information-seeking actions. As observations accumulate and beliefs sharpen, $I(b)$ decays toward zero and the pragmatic commit value dominates, shifting behavior toward goal realization. This transition requires no scheduling, annealing, or hyperparameter adjustment.

Our expanded baselines sharpen this claim. On the Tiger problem, both VFE and the same-horizon Planning agent achieve $\sim$99\% success with $\sim$4.2 listens. Here, the dynamic balance is matched by the reward structure itself: the $+10$ / $-100$ asymmetry makes premature commitment so costly that even a reward-only planner ``discovers'' the value of listening. The VFE mechanism produces the same behavior \emph{for different reasons}---the information gain term, rather than the reward penalty, drives continued listening.

The behavioral equivalence on Tiger obscures a deeper difference that emerges on multi-observation-action environments. On the Bandit ($K=4$ arms), VFE inspects 4.94 times and achieves $+6.46$ reward, while the same-horizon Planning agent inspects 3.26 times for only $+5.44$. The Planning agent lacks guidance on \emph{which} arm to inspect, whereas VFE's information gain term naturally directs inspection toward the most uncertain arms. This demonstrates that intrinsic exploration--exploitation balancing is most valuable when the action space includes multiple information-gathering options with differential informativeness.

The tuned Info Gain results expose a complementary pathology: when the exploration weight is optimized per environment, the agent systematically \emph{over-explores}, achieving 99+\% success but lowest reward on multiple environments. A fixed weight cannot simultaneously be appropriate across environments with different reward scales, demonstrating that additive exploration bonuses are fundamentally mismatched to the problem structure in ways that the EFE objective avoids.

\section{Future Work}

The experiments presented here validate EFE-as-$\rho$ across five structurally distinct environments with state spaces up to $N=16$. Several directions remain:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous state spaces}: Variational message passing or particle filter-based inference to extend beyond discrete state spaces, enabling application to robotics and continuous control.
    \item \textbf{LLM integration}: Using large language models as generative world models to parameterize transition dynamics and observation likelihoods, enabling EFE computation in semantic state spaces.
    \item \textbf{Computational scaling}: The recursive EFE evaluation has exponential cost in the planning horizon. Monte-Carlo tree search or amortized policy networks could enable deeper planning in larger state spaces.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical generative models}: Nested models with beliefs at multiple levels of abstraction, enabling epistemic foraging over both low-level states and high-level structure.
    \item \textbf{Multi-agent settings}: Whether constitutive epistemics produces beneficial properties (cooperation, communication) when multiple VFE agents interact.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Novelty Statement}

The novelty of this work lies in formally bridging the $\rho$-POMDP and active inference frameworks by substituting Expected Free Energy as the belief-dependent utility function $\rho$, and in rigorously characterizing \emph{where} this substitution helps and where it does not. While $\rho$-POMDPs provide the formal mechanism for belief-dependent optimization and active inference provides the principled objective, neither literature has systematically explored this integration with controlled baselines.

Our contribution is both theoretical---showing the substitution is coherent, tractable, and consistent with the pymdp reference implementation---and empirical. Through comparison against five baselines (Myopic, same-horizon Planning, untuned and tuned Information Gain, and pymdp's standard AIF agent), we establish that: (1) on simple observe-then-commit environments, VFE matches the best tuned alternatives without requiring per-environment weight selection; (2) on multi-observation-action environments requiring \emph{which}-information-to-gather decisions with sufficient planning depth, VFE significantly outperforms same-horizon reward-only planning ($+20.6$ pp on Bandit, $+9.1$ pp on Diagnosis at $H=3$); and (3) on very small state spaces, VFE's constitutive epistemic drive can over-explore. The scaling analysis confirms that both VFE and Planning provide growing advantage over myopic baselines as state spaces increase (from $+15.0$ pp at $N=2$ to $+38.8$ pp at $N=16$ for VFE over Myopic), while VFE's advantage over Planning specifically requires sufficient recursive depth ($H \geq 3$) to manifest---identifying structured uncertainty combined with deep planning as the regime where principled epistemic foraging is most valuable.
